when determining strength, consider:
- breaking back-bonding to central atom (less Lewis acidity)
- stability of conjugate and delocalisation/resonance to stabilise charge
	- charge density of atom which bears the charge (size)
	- electronegativity of atom which bears charge
- inductive effect of adjacent atoms that stabilise charge
- charge on $\ce{M+}$, which polarises O-H bond and repels the proton as it leaves
	- also the size of $\ce{M+}$
	- proton in hydroxyl bond can leave because:
		- the $\ce{M+}$ attracts $\ce{e-}$ density away from the ligand, pulling electron density away from the hydrogens in $\ce{H_{2}O}$
		- thus, the protons on the water are much more easily lost
		- greater charge density → more acidic complex


experimental tests to distinguish acid strength
- reaction with a reactive metal (e.g. $\ce{Zn(s), Mg(s)}$ or a carbonate/hydrogen carbonate) (vigorous/mild bubbling to produce $\ce{H_{2}(g) or CO_{2}(g)}$)
- temperature (high/low) when reacting with a strong base
- conductivity (high/low) for degree of ionisation (more free ions)
- pH, at same concentration, strong is lower
- pKa

experimental tests to distinguish base strength
- reaction with $\ce{Al(s)}$ strong/minimal production of $\ce{H_{2}(g)}$
- temperature (high/low) on reaction with strong acid
- conductivity (high/low) for degree of ionisation
- pH, at same concentration, strong is higher



oxo groups: $\ce{O=O}$

pauling's rules: estimates pKa of oxoacids:
$$
XO_{p}(OH)_{q}
$$
X: central atom
$O_{p}$: oxo group
$(OH)_{q}$: hydroxyl groups

$pKa\approx{8}-5p$

each successive deprotonation increases pKa by 5 units

$$
\begin{gather}
\ce{pH=-\log_{10}[H_{3}O+]}\\
\ce{pK=-\log_{10}K}
\end{gather}
$$

$\ce{H_{3}O+}$ is an oversimplification due to participation in hydrogen-bonding
- $\ce{H_{9}O_{4}+}$ more likely
- $\ce{H+}$ is surrounded by a cage of $\ce{H_{2}O}$ in a pentagonal dodecahedral arrangement

$$
\begin{gather}
\ce{HA(aq) +H_{2}O(l)<=>H_{3}O+(aq) +A-(aq)}&K_{a}=\frac{\ce{[H_{3}O+][A-]}}{\ce{[HA]}}
\end{gather}
$$
since concentration of water is assumed to be constant

therefore, when the concentration of water is not constant (i.e. all water is protonated or deprotonated, $\ce{pK_{a}}$ does not make sense)

> "A note on good practice: In precise work, $K_{a}$ is expressed in terms of the activity of X, $a(X)$, its effective thermodynamic concentration. The acidity constant is based on the assumption that solutions are sufficiently dilute for it to be permissible to write $a(H_{2}O)=1$."


and that the autoprotolysis of water can be neglected

similarly,

$$
\begin{gather}
\ce{B(aq) +H_{2}O(l)<=>HB+(aq) +OH-(aq)}&K_{b}=\ce{\frac{[HB+][OH-]}{[B]}}
\end{gather}
$$

autoprotolysis of water (self-ionisation)
$$
\begin{gather}
\ce{2H_{2}O(l)<=>H_{3}O+(aq) +OH-(aq)}&K_{w}=\ce{[H_{3}O+][OH-]}
\end{gather}
$$

experimental value at $T=25\pu{\degree C}:K_{w}=1.00\times{1}0^{-14}\pu{ M^{2}}$
- hence, only a very small fraction of water molecules are present as ions

hence, it is possible to find the acid/base dissociation constants by the other
$$
K_{a}K_{b}=K_w
$$

$$
\begin{gather}
-\log_{10}(K_{a}K_{b})=-\log_{10}K_{w}\\
pK_{a}+pK_{b}=pK_{w}
\end{gather}
$$

generally, $\ce{pK_{a_{2}}> pKa_{1}}$
